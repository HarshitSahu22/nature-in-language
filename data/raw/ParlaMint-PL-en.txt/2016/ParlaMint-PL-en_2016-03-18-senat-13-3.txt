The museum named after the Ulm family commemorates the tragic day of March 24, 1944, when the German gendarmes murdered 8 Jews and hid their Joseph Ulma and his wife, Victoria, who was the last month pregnant. They also killed six Ulm children, the oldest of whom, Stasia's daughter, was only 8 years old. It should be added that since the end of 1942, the Ulm family has reported to Germany the so-called navynny policeman from Lańcuta. The day before the Ulm house, five German policemen and several policemen arrived. They were led by Lieutenant Eilert Dieken. First Jews were murdered, then Joseph and Victoria. Then Dieken decided to kill the children. 17 people lost their lives. The Ulm were buried in the cemetery in Markowa, while the Jews were buried in the cemetery in Jagielle, the burial site of 41 other victims of the Holocaust from Markowa. In honor of the heroic Ulm family, I will mention that the Polish Sejm in a special resolution honored all the compatriots who risked their lives to save the Jews who were doomed. He particularly mentioned those who died at the hands of the German occupier in retaliation for their assistance. The museum opened yesterday in Markowa is intended to inspire state and local authorities to continue commemorating the actions of Poles saving Jews and promoting this knowledge also outside Poland. This task is all the more important because the world does not know the realities that prevailed on Polish lands during the tragic years of the war 1939-1945, and historical ignorance with which we meet abroad strikes the good name of our homeland. This is demonstrated by the lyingly repeated naming of the Polish concentration camps. So it is important to make everyone aware of the hatred that German invaders have destroyed then the Polish state and culture, murdered the leaders, used terror and committed crimes against the civilian population. What was most cruel, unlike occupied western Europe in Polish lands, was the death penalty for even the slightest help to Jews. Despite the threat of death, many Poles decided to help persecuted Jews. It is estimated that tens of thousands of people have survived because of this help. This is proved by the number of Poles awarded with the title "The Righteous Among the Nations" and the number of trees planted at the Yad Vashem Institute in Jerusalem. In 1995, Victoria and Józef Ulm were also honored posthumously as the Righteous Among the Nations. In 2003 in the Diocese of Przemysl began their beatification process, and in 2004 a monument was unveiled in Markowa, which reminds us of these heroic people. In 2010, President Lech Kaczyński marked them with the Commander's Cross of the Order of the Rebirth of Poland, stressing that their entire family became a symbol of Polish sacrifice and martyrdom during the German occupation. Thank you for your attention. [[Applause]]
