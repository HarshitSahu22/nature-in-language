Madam President, ladies and gentlemen, today's news is that an aesthetic institute in Asti is looking for an aesthetic woman who is not overweight, who can work 11 hours in a row and who has no family problems that might hinder work. You look for, you know, a young woman, skinny and lonely. While it is true that being lean and alone is sometimes a choice made in full right, respectable as any other choice (as long as it is legal and civil), it is also true that it is bitter that certain characteristics end up in a job offer. It is understandable that an aesthetic center and weight loss wants to give the best possible perception of itself to the customer and that therefore the owner or owner can give preference to beautiful staff, able to become aspirational for the customer and to reflect what in theory can be done for him. I understand the problems of communication and marketing, the competition and the desire to make money and success with your company. In fact, I'm not mad at this aesthetic center in particular. If anything, I think it is precisely the cultural paradigm that has ended up in a dark street, often not dignified, especially for women. Beyond the aesthetic factor that - I realize - is inevitably something that changes the perception that others have of us (including that of employers), the most difficult part to accept is the one that concerns the family. What does it matter how I cope efficiently with 11 hours of work? The only thing that really matters is if I can. I imagine that the employer may fear that, if one has family problems such as children or dependent elders, once the contract has been signed, he may use terms and quirks to be paid and to tear up a few hours for care or simply to find himself with an inefficient employee. However, this risk exists and there is always of course. Women have a great ability to deal with and manage more than one problem at a time, they often have a greater willpower and resistance than men and, above all, they have the same right to work and compose their own happiness that is reserved for men. However, males are rarely asked to be fit or if they have children or elderly dependent: mostly they are asked for competence and dedication. However, I would like to reflect on one point: if it is true that it is a specific characteristic of women to become pregnant and give birth, it is also the desire not to cancel themselves in that process, to give the best of themselves and to prove that they are still a productive, competent and present person. We may also find that even in an aesthetic center, even a more fat and maternal woman can have its own value, perhaps just the value that she has achieved thanks to children or the care she has given to the elderly. The risk of quitting the value of people's history and focusing on how effectively they can undo their work is, frankly, a little scary. I therefore hope that we will return to giving centrality to the person, to his being and not just to thinking about productivity. This issue, moreover, also emerged yesterday in 11a The Commission during the informal hearing on the examination of the deal given on the impact on the labour market of the fourth industrial revolution, in fact, we raised precisely the issue of the centrality of the person within the world of work.
