Madam President, ladies and gentlemen, I leave you in the evening - although sad - so sweet for us living, with the waxy light that the neighborhood in the shadows is caught. And it sommoves it, it makes it bigger, empty around and farther it rekindles it. This - ladies and gentlemen - was Pier Paolo Pasolini, whose fortieth death occurs in these days. It was a step of a famous poem, dedicated to Antonio Gramsci and the place where he is buried, where his narrative and lexical talent and his evocative ability emerge, certainly helped by the beauty of the city in which we are. Many other passages of poetry and prose we could, however, now quote to remember him, as - for example - his invectives on the columns of the daily newspapers (published in collections called, not by chance, "Scritti corsari" and "Lettere lutherane" (Luther's Letters), beginning with that "io" that he used on the "Corriere della Sera" (Corriere della Sera) to denounce those who - in his opinion - were the moral agents of the fascist massacres of Brescia and Bologna. It happened on November 14, 1974, just a year before he was killed, under circumstances never fully clarified. Pier Paolo Pasolini taught us a method; he explained to us what democracy, freedom of opinion and expression are all about. He showed us freedom, period. Today is very popular a term - caste - which, in the end, is the vulgarization of what Pasolini called the Palace. Today we all enjoy a freedom of costumes, a breadth and a variety of artistic and political manifestations because Pasolini, with his insatiable desire to live, has opened the way for us. In his existence too short he wanted to go through all the ravines of life. He has searched all literary forms, from dialectal poetry to novel - let's think about "A violent life" and "Boys of life" - from the theater to the critics (remember the voluminous "Descriptions of descriptions"). He modernized the cinema in an impressive way, starting from the neo-realist furrow and also bringing back a criminal trial for vilipendio of the religion of State (for the film "La ricotta"). From his interlocutors he wanted everything: wisdom, honest confrontation, thickness. He was as demanding to others as he was to himself. The former Christian Democracy demonized him because he was homosexual and for the Communist Party itself he was not a Beniamino: the PCI in 1949 had come to expel him for that reason and considered him heterodox. Pasolini, I said, was insatiable, a voracious observer of society, of urbanism and of the outskirts, therefore also of the world in which I grew up, at the gates of Milan. For this reason, in 1968 - I was really a child - he argued with the prevailing opinion on the left about the clashes in Valle Giulia, Rome, among the educated students and, in a certain way, privileged and the children of the South, recruited to the Police. Then, of course, as always, right-wing and hypocritically well-thought-out ideology exploited that position. Its history is therefore tremendously current, because it speaks to us of social equality and of the diversity of personal conditions, of intimate freedom and public anti-conformism. He asks us to be genuine with ourselves and with the country. Forty years have passed and many politicians today do not have that age: they tweet and post cheerfully, trampling on the anguish and complexity of life that Pasolini instead took care of. The memory of him, however, is not regretful, he would not appreciate it. Pasolini was a critical point, a precursor of the times and would be enthusiastic about the timing of politics and culture today: of course, he wouldn't be afraid of it; only, perhaps, would he put his depth, the courage to challenge the strong powers, the real ones, and his sharpness of gaze. Today he would address his "I know" to the grittyness of certain economic analyses, to the ideology of financial rigour, to the political opportunism and to the consorteries of the mafias and corruption, and to this voice of his we would need very much.
