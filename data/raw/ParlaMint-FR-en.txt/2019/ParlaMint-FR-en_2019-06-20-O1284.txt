However, Mayotte cannot be built without the trust of the Mahorese and Mahorais in their elected representatives, in the state, and, above all, in the future. Trust in security, first. I often say that there is no development without security, and no security without development. Trust in the state on the spot, then. I chose Dominique Sorain, who at the time led my cabinet, to be prefect, government delegate in Mayotte. Working substantively, with a strengthened team, in all the priority areas for the Mahorese, it has enabled the development of this plan. He set a framework and a method. The framework, first of all, is to meet the challenges of this territory, the needs of its people and its representatives. The plan for the future of Mayotte deals not only with security, the fight against immigration, access to care but also with administrative catch-up, with the creation of a full-function rectorate, a regional health agency that is no longer under the supervision of La Réunion, and a dedicated regional employment department. These three old requests, which you, like other elected representatives, Mr. Rapporteur, have never been heard. This plan also deals with the support of local actors in the conduct of water access and social housing projects. Its 53 commitments and 125 actions are closely monitored. As I had made the commitment, I myself went to the site to present the first assessment in April, eleven months after its adoption. The Transparency website allows each of our citizens to make sure that this plan is progressing. The method, then, is concertation and listening. This plan is a document not only from the Government or the State but from all the public authorities, because the territorial authorities and parliamentarians who so wished have been involved in its preparation. Around the Prefect, the Government's delegate, an inter-ministerial team has come to strengthen the State's services on the spot. The team was in direct contact with elected officials, social actors and all components of civil society. It is probably for these reasons that the result is up to the expectations of our fellow citizens over the defined period. This is my method, and you know it: that of consultation, co-construction, transparency and listening to the ground. Only in this way can we build a real policy, a real strategy to meet the expectations of the population. This strategy will also be part of Mayotte's convergence plan under the February 2017 law on real overseas equality adopted here unanimously. The plan sets out the strategic directions and targets for 2030 for infrastructure, education, economics and health. It was co-built by local actors and adopted in December 2018 in Mayotte. But this strategy only makes sense in concrete projects that respond well to the needs of our fellow citizens. This is the subject of the convergence and transformation contracts, which are under negotiation and finalisation. For Mayotte, as for most other overseas departments and regions, they will be signed on July 8, as announced by the Prime Minister in the Senate and in the Assembly. The President of the Republic hoped that their duration would be set at four years, from 2019 to 2022, thus giving a realistic course to the projects and allowing the population to measure very concretely the fulfilment of the commitments made. Developed according to the method I recalled, the contract for Mayotte involves the state, the department, the community of agglomeration of Dembeni-Mamoudzou and the other four intercommunalities of the island. It has been the subject of several round trips between the main actors to reach the best possible document – at least I hope so because I will only discover the final document in a few days. It will be signed very soon. I can already state that it will financially transcribe the State's commitments under Mayotte's plan for the future: EUR 119.5 million for water and sanitation; EUR 476 million for school infrastructure; EUR 172 million for the hospital centre; €136 million for social housing; EUR 160 million for road investments, including the circumvention of Mamoudzou, to name but a few of the main financial resources. Its total amount is expected to be around €1.6 billion, of which 1.1 billion are state credits, or on average €275 million per year. This contract covers an even wider scope than Mayotte's plan for the future, since it also deals with the prevention of natural risks, climate change and the energy transition, higher education, cultural infrastructure and support for the international opening of companies. Mr President, Mr Kamardine, you already see that the ambitions of this convergence and transformation contract for the next four years go beyond those of your proposed law. We must live up to the hopes of the Mahorese and the Mahorese. I know we will work together to achieve this. You will agree, the answers to the needs for catching up of Mayotte's infrastructures are well reflected in this contract. They take into account the territorial capacity for achievement, and this is an important point, because it is not enough to hope for everything right away. The narrowness of the territory, the difficulties in identifying and disposing of land and the availability of both public and private actors lead to the need to set priorities, accompany and closely monitor the good progress of the projects. It is not enough to sign contracts: as I have seen in recent years at the head of the department, it is necessary to accompany the implementation of the projects. Funding for this convergence and transformation contract is assured: it will be included in the finance bill for 2020. Ladies and gentlemen, as you can see, the state has spared no effort to act, neither in the short term, in the urgency – it was necessary – nor to ensure the development of Mayotte in the medium and long term. Your proposal for a law, Mr rapporteur, also concerns social equality. It is a subject that is of particular interest to me and you can count on me to move it forward. We will probably have an opportunity to discuss it a little later. Social benefits are not the same in Mayotte as in the other overseas departments or in Hexagon, and that is not fair. This is due to a more recent administrative history, which, however, is not a sufficient argument to justify the persistence of such inequality. This is also due to a delicate balance between the capacity of the local economic fabric and the legitimate expectations of the population. In order to increase social security benefits, social contributions must also be increased. That's how our system works. And increasing social contributions means increasing the cost of work, which can penalise businesses and employment in a territory where the unemployment rate already stands at 35%. This means not that we must not do it, but that we must act in a rational way, just for all, and accompanied. On my April trip, I announced that a timetable for the evolution of benefits would be given to the Mahorese and the Mahorese before the end of the year – this had never been done – because we must give realistic, but ambitious prospects. You are also proposing, Mr rapporteur, that the communities should be better equipped.
