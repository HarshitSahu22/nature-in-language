ParlaMint-FR_2018-02-07-O1137.u234	Broadly inspired by American policy, but not a literal translation, group action was introduced into consumer law and competition law by the law of 17 March 2014, known as the Hamon Act. The French legislator did not only intend to comply with the obligation to harmonise practices at European level, but wanted to give Internet users powerful tools to control and sanction e-commerce. The development of e-consumption – or more precisely the new forms of consumption, communication, information and research – justifies that collective action should not be confined to the particular field of consumer law. That is why it is also included in the law of 26 January 2016 on the modernization of our health system – and therefore in the law of health. Nor is it surprising that it was subsequently defined in a legal and procedural framework common to all matters of law, on the basis of which such an appeal is now possible. In addition, its scope was extended from 2016 to the fight against discrimination and the protection of the environment, before being today extended to the protection of personal data through the inclusion of a new Article 43a in the Data Protection and Freedoms Act promulgated in 1978. The group action provides for a remedy for material damage. It allows several persons who consider themselves to be victims of the same harm caused by the same professional to regroup and take legal action with one voice. With regard to the processing of personal data, it has an exceptionally distinct purpose. It also refers not to material reparation for the damage suffered, but only to the cessation of the breaches of the law by the perpetrator. ParlaMint-FR_2018-02-07-O1137.u338	While GDPR is a step forward in the current opacity of processing our digital life, it is only a step. It allows each individual to identify and control the flow of his or her digital data, but he or she is silent about the heritage and moral value of the data. Yet we are living a moment in the history of the world where, beyond body and mind, women and men, who now use interactive and intelligent tools, generate information, digitize their daily lives, record in digital form the details of their lives, whether they relate to health, transportation, activities, exchanges or transactions. This information is compiled and interpreted by algorithms developed by managers, often US GAFAMs – Google, Apple, Facebook, Amazon and Microsoft – or Asian BATXs – Alibaba, Tencent, Huawei and Xiaomi – who use them for commercial purposes, as de facto owners, to target ads. If there is a patent or copyright right, there is no right attached to personal digital data. The aim of this amendment is to restore to everyone what belongs to him: the enjoyment of moral rights in his digital data, a French specificity. Everyone will have the opportunity to bequeath them to his heirs, to make them freely available to the community, but above all he will have authority over his digital integrity. Awareness of the value of these data, which goes beyond the question of their market value, is a key cultural issue of our century. By allowing our digital productions to be plundered freely, without discussion of their property, we risk a levelling or even erasing of global diversity. Many objects and images of missing civilizations now protected in the name of heritage preservation and exhibited in museum windows have never been conceived or realized by anything other than the necessities and routines of everyday life. We must consider our digital data to be of the same order. If we dared today to give our fellow citizens a moral right to their digital data, we would take a small step, but certainly also a giant leap towards the debate we will not escape: to whom will our memories of the future belong?
