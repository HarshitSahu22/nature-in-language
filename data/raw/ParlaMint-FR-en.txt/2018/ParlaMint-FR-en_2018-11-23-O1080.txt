Therefore, acquittal is often experienced as a second trauma to the victim, especially since it has not only the effect of innocating the alleged aggressor; In the eyes of society, it is also tantamount to designating the victim herself as a potential liar. This is particularly devastating in cases of sexual violence, sometimes difficult to prove materially. One must imagine the distress of a victim who sees the prosecution demanding a very heavy sentence and then not appealing the acquittal handed down by the court. These are not isolated cases: between 2003 and 2005, appeal courts had to try only 76 cases in which acquittal had been handed down, while the number of acquittals per year exceeded 200. There is no doubt, therefore, that the majority of acquittals are not followed by a request for appeal by the Public Prosecutor's Office. The victim's right of appeal in the event of a release would also be the natural and coherent extension of the rights currently enjoyed by victims. In fact, the victim initiates the investigation by lodging a complaint, may overrule the prosecutor's dismissal by way of a civil suit and has the capacity to appeal the order of dismissal of the investigating judge. This is why eminent judges such as Claude Mathon, Advocate General of the Court of Cassation, have been able to defend the idea that the victim's right of appeal in the event of release or acquittal would bring our legal system into line.
