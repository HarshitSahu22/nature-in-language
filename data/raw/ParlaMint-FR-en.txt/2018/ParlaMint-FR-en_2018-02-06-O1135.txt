The law in force already addresses the legitimate concern for the control of the activities of intelligence services, be it the use of intelligence techniques, controlled by the CNCTR, as well as its result in terms of information collected and processed, this control of the files being carried out by the CNIL. The intelligence files constitute a subset of the so-called sovereignty files: those that are relevant to State security, defence and public security and that are mentioned in article 26 of the Data Protection and Freedoms Act. These files are not subject to the GDPR or the directive that this bill intends to transcribe. Because of their very nature and the purposes they serve, certain rights are not recognized by individuals. The right of access to the data contained in these processes is thus excluded. However, any person who questions the presence in these files of data concerning him or her may apply to the CNIL, which will carry out the verifications in its place: this is called the right of indirect access, as defined in Article 41 of the Data Protection and Freedoms Act. The main features of these intelligence files – the purposes of the file, the type of data collected, the recipients of the information, the absence of interconnection – are defined in a regulatory text, a decree in Council of State issued after the advice of the CNIL. But, for obvious reasons of confidentiality and in order not to question their very purposes, most of them are exempt from publication, as permitted by Article III of Article 26 of the Computer Law and Freedoms. A decree of 15 May 2007 lists the 14 files concerned. The possibility for the CNIL to carry out a posteriori check on documents and on the spot, full and complete, is excluded only for eight of these fourteen files, in accordance with Article IV of Article 44 of the Data Protection and Freedoms Act. These are only the most sensitive intelligence files for which the possibility of copying any document or information and of reviewing the architecture of technical tools is excluded, at the risk of seriously jeopardizing the modalities of action of the intelligence services, but also the link of trust with the foreign partner services. Only with the express agreement of a partner can information be made available to a third party. These eight files are the responsibility of the DGSE, for two of them, the DGSI, the Directorate of Military Intelligence, for two of them also, the Directorate of Intelligence and Defence Security, the Intelligence Service of the Police Prefecture and the Customs Service. While CNIL cannot implement full control of these files under ordinary law, they are subject to several controls. The information contained therein is often derived from very strictly controlled intelligence techniques. The implementation of an intelligence technique requires, as you know, prior authorization from the Prime Minister after the advice of an independent administrative authority, the CNCTR. The Commission shall also carry out a second inspection once the information technology has been implemented in order to ensure compliance with the conditions laid down in the authorisation. This ex post check is carried out for each service two to three times a month, in a very thorough manner. It is complemented by a demanding dialogue maintained by the CNCTR with the intelligence services on any point of interpretation of the strict legal framework developed by the legislator in the Act of 24 July 2015. The information collected after using a technique can then be transcribed into an intelligence service file. But at the outset, we must not forget, the creation of this file is subject to a preliminary examination by the CNIL, then by the Council of State, which, in order to authorize the creation by decree of this processing, ensures the legality of its main characteristics – type of data collected, methods of preservation, possibilities of crossing. Once this file has been created, and within the framework of the indirect right of access referred to above, CNIL visits each of the intelligence services three to four times a year to verify, for the benefit of the persons who seize it, that the data held, if any, by the services are necessary and comply with the characteristics initially defined in the regulatory text for which CNIL has given its opinion. This control is subsequently the subject of extensive exchanges between the intelligence service concerned and CNIL in order, where appropriate, to correct or erase data that would not be relevant. Finally, a person who believes that he or she is known to an intelligence service may, without any precondition other than suspicion, seize a specialized training course of the Council of State entitled to national defence secrecy, in order to obtain the erasure of the data concerning him or her, if they are unlawfully held by the intelligence services. In the course of this appeal, CNIL systematically intervenes and produces the information it has collected in the exercise of its right of indirect access. As you can see, ladies and gentlemen, this plurality of controls responds to a specific logic defined at the time of the adoption of the intelligence law of July 2015. As such, the CNCTR is responsible for controlling the collection of raw intelligence data, which are collected when an intelligence technology is used. For its part, CNIL is competent to control the conditions under which the data collected is stored and, above all, used in a file. This dividing line, desired by the legislator, is essential since the activities involved by the intelligence services are not the same. While both CNCTR and CNIL members and officers are entitled to the secrecy of national defence, the principle of intelligence partitioning, applied in the day-to-day operation of the services as well as in their relations with their foreign partners, explains and justifies the balance found, which, I believe, reconciles the protection of freedoms, including the right to privacy, and the requirements related to the preservation of the fundamental interests of the nation.
