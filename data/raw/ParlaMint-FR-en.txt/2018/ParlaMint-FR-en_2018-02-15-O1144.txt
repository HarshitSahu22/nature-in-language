These criteria would be too imprecise to meet the requirement laid down by the Court of Justice of the European Union in its judgment of 15 March 2017. The Court also stated – in the same judgment – that "retention of applicants, constituting a serious interference with their right to freedom, is subject to strict guarantees, namely the presence of a legal basis, clarity, predictability, accessibility and protection against arbitrariness". This case law is in line with that of the European Court of Human Rights, which had already stated that in order to comply with Article 5(1) of the European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms, "a national law authorizing deprivation of liberty must be sufficiently accessible, precise and predictable in its application". However, the criteria for concealment relating to the migration route, the family situation or previous asylum applications seem to be too vaguely formulated to meet these requirements, especially in the light of the trauma suffered by these foreigners. Socially and humanly, this addition is also a problem. It does not take into account the possible psychological distress of foreigners under Dublin procedure. The fear of being transferred to their country of origin, the lack of knowledge of the procedure, the psychological shock may lead to the failure to disclose certain information, without necessarily the existence of a willful intention. The addition of the Senate is therefore neither relevant nor useful, since identity concealments are already likely to characterize the significant risk of leakage.
