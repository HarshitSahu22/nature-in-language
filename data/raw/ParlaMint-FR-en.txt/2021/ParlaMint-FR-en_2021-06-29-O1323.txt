Why legislate on religious neutrality in election campaigns? Since secularism was rooted in the French Republic, a republican tradition has made consensus for decades among political forces, including those claiming their religious roots: the refusal to use any religious sign for electoral propaganda purposes. This consensus has been respected up to the last few years. Voices were expressed, and not least, to warn about attempts to bring religions into the realm of elective democracy. If it becomes necessary to legislate, it is because the election by universal suffrage is no longer immune to separatist aims which divert the absolute principle of freedom of opinion and conscience to instill, in the very foundation of representative democracy, the poison of electoral communalism and, as the President of the Republic said to the Walls, the poison of political Islamism. Whatever the spirit of secularism generally displayed by political formations, they cannot, on their own, guarantee that the electoral act remains immune to religious demands. How, then, can the constitutional principle of freedom of opinion and conscience be reconciled in law with the principle of the same constitutional value of preventing breaches of public order? I propose to extend to the use of religious emblems during the electoral act the measure in force for the national emblem: the ban. The legislator, after consulting the Council of State, has also forced the use of the three colours, blue, white and red, in this context. Why would the ban, which applies to the national emblem, not be for an ostensible religious emblem or sign? Just as the use of the national emblem may alter the sincerity of a vote, that of denominational signs, in addition to raising a problem of compliance with article 3 of the Constitution, is likely to disturb public order.
