ParlaMint-FR_2021-05-18-O1275.u494	When you took office, Mr. Keeper of the Seals, you announced that you wanted to launch, "at last, the long-awaited reform of the Public Prosecutor's Office" and want to "improve the justice of our country". Unfortunately, our hopes were quickly disappointed. First of all by the Government's method, which, it is true, is still a little the same. The reform we are looking at is in fact challenged by almost all of the justice professionals who denounce the lack of consultations prior to the elaboration of a "cradle - tote" reform, "without ambition", "unwritten". They consider that this umpteenth bill purporting to reconcile citizens with their judicial system is in total mismatch with the reality of the needs of justice. However, as the president of the Union Syndicat des Magistratures points out, "trust is not decreed". Disamour is very real since, according to a survey published by the CEVIPOF last February, only 48% of our citizens say they have confidence in the judicial institution. Admittedly, a number of measures in the text are progressing. First of all, I am thinking of the possibility of filming the trials for reasons of public interest and of broadcasting them later on television. This measure may present educational virtues, provided that these broadcasts are not the occasion of a race to hear, which would lead to favouring major criminal trials or those involving public figures. That is why we propose that they be entrusted to the public service. I am also thinking of setting the length of the preliminary investigation, which will ensure fair justice – although the government machinery can be improved – and of strengthening the lawyer's professional secrecy. In this regard, we welcome the fact that the guarantees of professional secrecy have been extended, in committee, to the work of counseling lawyers. Finally, I will mention the reform of the status of detained workers, which sometimes gives rise to abuses of all kinds, particularly with regard to remuneration and working time. We will therefore remain very attentive to the decree that will be issued by the Government, even if you are right on one point: it is urgent to act. It is well known that prisoners who have worked or trained during their imprisonment are those with the lowest likelihood of re-incarceration. However, how can you claim to reconcile the people with their judicial system when you generalize departmental criminal courts to the detriment of assize courts – which removes the people from the decisions made on their behalf? How can you claim to restore public confidence in the judicial institution when the derisory means granted to the justice system, especially civil ones, prevent professionals from meeting the deadlines, which contributes to popular exasperation? How can you claim that you are worried about the fate of the most fragile when your bill ignores the litigation justice of everyday life, even though the diversion and dematerialization measures taken in recent years have further removed the litigants from the local courts? Civil justice renders 2.2 million decisions a year, when criminal justice renders 800,000. It is therefore at the centre of French life. Yet it remains the great forgotten of the reforms. The announcement of the hiring of a thousand contractors to reduce the processing times of civil justice is not enough to reassure us: 1.3 million court cases are pending, with average processing times of fourteen months at first instance and 17 months at appeal. Even if the 1,000 legal assistants and registry reinforcements who will be taking up their posts by the beginning of September will have the task of removing magistrates and clerks from the most time-consuming tasks, training contractors takes time and it is common – as we see regularly – that the latter, who are poorly paid, resign before the end of their three-year contract. Only through the establishment of posts of magistrates and clerks would civil justice be able to render intelligible decisions within a reasonable time. These contradictions will not have escaped anyone – not even you, minister. That is why, on this text, which, despite a few steps in the right direction, is currently missing out on the real stakes, we reserve our vote, waiting to see what progress our work will make.
