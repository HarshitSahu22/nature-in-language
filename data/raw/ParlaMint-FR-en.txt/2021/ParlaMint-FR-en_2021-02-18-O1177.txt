Pauline's life, like that of so many others, has been chambouled since she suffered from a form of long covid, a pathology recognized in August by the World Health Organization – WHO – and characterized by persistent symptoms several weeks, even months, after the contraction of the disease. Among the so many victims of the covid-19, there are those who unfortunately died as a result of the disease. There are these many patients whose condition required care in the hospital and in resuscitation service, who suffer from physical, cardiac, muscle, but also serious psychological and psychiatric sequelae. To these serious patients are added other victims, less known and less visible, most often women and many young people, whose daily lives have been disrupted since the infection. These are the victims of so-called "long covid", characterized by persistent symptoms that are particularly disabling in the first place, including rapid shortness of breath, chronic fatigue and exercise disadaptation, as well as cognitive disorders, anosmia or agueuses. These patients have often had relatively benign forms of covid-19, but suffer from long-term polymorphic symptoms – chest pain, palpitations, headache – as well as dysfunction and inability to do several things at the same time. Many of them, due to persistent attention disorders, are unable to return to work. Some have come to the end of their rights and may see great financial difficulties added to physical and psychological suffering. Long-term affection – ALD – is not recognized by many by health insurance and the care they need can be costly and complex to obtain. Experts also report particularly alarming psychiatric sequelae of covid-19 in many patients: anxiety, insomnia, depression, post-traumatic syndromes, obsessive symptoms. According to Professor Marion Leboyer, the psychiatric consequences of the covid-19 are before us. If it is still too early to give precise figures, it is likely that these forms of long covid concern several hundred thousand people in France. WHO estimates the number of such victims between 10 and 20 million worldwide. However, the mechanism for redressing the harm suffered by the victims of the covid-19 provided for by the decree of 14 September 2020 is far from meeting the challenges. For caregivers, first of all, contrary to what had been announced repeatedly by the Government, including in this House, the recognition of covid-19 as an occupational disease is neither automatic nor systematic. In order to receive compensation, caregivers and caregivers must have acute respiratory conditions, have recourse to oxygen therapy or any other form of respiratory assistance and have received medical treatment within 14 days of exposure to the virus. These criteria are particularly restrictive and irrelevant in that, as we have seen, the cardiac, neurological or cerebral sequelae of covid-19 affect many patients initially with mild forms of the disease. For other workers and those whose pathology does not correspond to the very strict criteria I have just stated, the recognition of covid-19 as an occupational disease is a true journey of the combatant. In early February, trade union organisations reported only 200 recognitions under the new table 100 of occupational diseases and the supplementary procedure. These victims are required to prove the existence of a "direct and essential link" between their work and infection with the virus; This link is particularly difficult to establish in the context of an epidemic like the one we know. The existence of a two-speed system is also the subject of a serious misunderstanding on the part of the victims. It shows a form of contempt for the commitment of many workers, such as home helpers, cashiers, law enforcement and security officers, teachers, cleanliness workers and traders, and so many others on the front in the midst of a health crisis. While they often suffer from the same sequelae, how can they explain that they are not placed on an equal footing with caregivers and assimilated? Concentrated around recognition as an occupational disease alone, the existing compensation scheme also leaves many victims behind: self-employed workers, among whom a very small minority, about 20,000, are insured against the risk of occupational accidents-illnesses at work – AT-MP; "environmental" victims, who have been contaminated outside the professional setting; volunteers who come to help in solidarity associations; pensioners since, as I recall, 90% of those who died were over 65 years of age; residents in EHPAD, who paid the highest tribute to the crisis with more than 30,000 deaths out of the 83,000 deaths to date; but also the relatives of the deceased. The compensation currently proposed is largely inadequate. Only flat-rate, it does not take into account the many physical, economic and moral harm suffered by victims. We must act in the face of the distress of these many people who call us, beyond speeches, to take action. Reparation for the harm suffered by these victims is a duty, in several ways. Beyond the search for responsibility, the establishment of an ambitious system of compensation for the victims of the covid-19 is primarily a matter of national solidarity. The first is to recognize the commitment of all those who have taken many risks to ensure the care of the sick and the continuity of the nation. It is also a question of taking into account the unbearable suffering of these victims, many who now depend on their loved ones for all the gestures of everyday life. For all these reasons, ladies and gentlemen, together with Christian Hutin and members of the Socialist and related groups, we propose the creation of a fund to compensate the victims of the covid-19. Our bill opens up compensation to all serious victims, regardless of whether the disease is of professional origin or not. Taking into account those left behind by the existing scheme, the fund aims to provide full compensation for the harm suffered by all victims with temporary or permanent sequelae of covid-19, whether those affected by severe sequelae resulting from serious forms of the disease or those affected by persistent symptoms characteristic of long forms of covid-19. The remedy that we are considering is also for those entitled to the benefit of those who have unfortunately died as a result of the disease. We have not forgotten them and we propose the establishment of a full reparation, aimed at enabling the victim to live in conditions as close as possible to the situation prior to the disease. The compensation fund for victims of covid-19 is based on that of victims of asbestos, whose model is now widely tested. The creation of such a fund has many advantages. It significantly reduces the risk of litigation, which adds concern and uncertainty to victims who have already experienced the disease. Moreover, it is particularly protective: since the victim refers to damage recognized by the fund, he or she can obtain compensation without having to prove a fault. The value of setting up an indemnification fund also lies in the speed and simplicity of the indemnification procedure. The Fund would be required to submit a proposal for reparation within six months of receipt of the request. It would also centralize all applications and act as a one-stop shop; Thus, in the case of a pathology developed in a professional context, the fund would turn itself to the potentially competent AT-MP schemes, rather than asking victims to follow a procedure in advance. Finally, the method of financing the fund we are proposing seems to us to be particularly balanced. Based mainly on a contribution from the State and the AT-MP branch of social security, it involves employers and the State, in the name of their respective responsibilities and national solidarity. Our bill is receiving support and a great expectation not only from workers' unions, but also from the victims' associations that we have heard. The principle of setting up a compensation fund is also upheld by the National Academy of Medicine. We do not oppose compensation for the various serious forms of the disease and the improvement of medical follow-up, on the contrary. As our colleague Caroline Fiat said last week, in order to begin to heal, we must first be recognised.
