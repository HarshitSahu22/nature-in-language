ParlaMint-FR_2022-02-01-O1142.u423	To debate the European Union's energy strategy requires reference to its history. Indeed, Europe has built itself around energy. At the end of a second world war that bruised the continent, a shared ambition took shape: to make any new conflict between France and Germany as unthinkable as possible. It materialised in 1951 by the creation of the first supranational institution of our continent, the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC), followed seven years later by the creation of another body, the European Atomic Energy Community (EAEC). Security is the common denominator of this political union around energy, as the Suez Canal crisis revealed the difficulties of oil supply at a time when coal was gradually being replaced. Political Europe was born. It has since adopted an operating treaty, still in force today. Article 194 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) sets out the framework for European energy policy, based on three principles: the functioning of the internal market, the requirement to preserve the environment and solidarity between Member States. In line with these three principles, the European energy policy is divided into four objectives: securing the Union's supply, supporting energy efficiency, developing renewable energy and developing network interconnections. However, Article 194 TFEU reveals a contradiction. Despite the desire to build a common energy policy, Member States remain free to exploit their energy resources as they see fit and can freely choose their energy sources and supplies.
