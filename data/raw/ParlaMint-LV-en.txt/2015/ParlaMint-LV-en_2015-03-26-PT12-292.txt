Article 64 of the Satversme of the Republic of Latvia is to be read in conjunction with Article 1 of the Satversme, which states that Latvia is an independent democratic republic. ‘ It follows from this Article a number of principles of the rule of law, including the principle of the separation of powers of the State. The purpose of the division of power is to ensure the rights and freedoms of a person in order to prevent the replacement of a democratic, legal state model by an authoritarian regime or a one-person self-government. Consequently, the principle of separation of powers is based on ensuring that the political influence on the life of the State and society is shared and that the methods of exercising power are balanced. This principle is reflected in the division of powers between the State and the legislative, executive and judicial bodies, which are exercised by independent and autonomous bodies. This guarantees balance, mutual control and contributes to moderate power.” ( I have just quoted paragraph 1 of the Opinion of the Constitutional Court of 1 October 1999 in the case No. 0305(99). “The division of competences of state authorities as the basis of the principle of division of power established by the Constitution has been reinstated in the norms of the institutional part of the Constitution.” ( reference is made to paragraph 6.2 of the judgment of the Constitutional Court in case No 2006-12-01.) According to Article 64 of the Satversme, the right to legislation belongs to the Saeima, as well as to the people in accordance with the procedure and amount laid down in the Constitution. In Latvia, the Cabinet of Ministers may issue an external legislative act in the event that the legislator has formulated a special delegation to issue such an act and has established limits of competence of the executive power. The terms of the delegation of the special mandate are regulated in general by Section 31 of the Cabinet of Ministers Equipment Law. The condition that the law should include both the power to issue regulations and the main directions of these regulations is derived from the requirement that the legislator would decide only the most important issues of public life. The Constitutional Court has already indicated in advance that under the administration the Cabinet of Ministers can issue only legislative acts subordinate to the law, therefore the rules must comply with the law. The rules are issued to help implement the laws and this is achieved by specifying the rules. When issuing regulations, the Cabinet of Ministers may not include in them such norms which are not to be regarded as aids for the implementation of the norm of the law. Thus, the regulations of the Cabinet of Ministers, issued on the basis of a special delegation, form part of the regulatory enactments, which originated not in the drafting of the law, but in its implementation. The content of these regulations consists mainly of procedural norms, which act as an instrument of reinstatement of previously established rights. In certain cases, the content of regulations of the Cabinet of Ministers could also be formed by material norms, but they should be adopted on the basis of a special authorisation of the legislator. Finally, I would remind you that Section 32(2) of the Constitutional Court Law provides that the judgment of the Constitutional Court and its interpretation of the relevant legal norm are mandatory for all state and municipal institutions. This means that amendments submitted by Mr Šadurska and supported by the Commission not only contradict the provisions of Section 31(1) of the Cabinet of Ministers’ Equipment Law, but also the interpretation of the relevant legal norm in the judgments of the Constitutional Court of 24 November 2005 and 9 October 2007. Thus, Article 2 of the draft law is presented in contradiction with the principle of division of state power, which arises from Article 1 and Article 64 of the Satversme, as well as includes such restrictions which are prohibited by Article 116 of the Satversme.
