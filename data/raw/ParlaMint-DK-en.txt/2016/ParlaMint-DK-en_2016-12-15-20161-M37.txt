There may be derived or dynamic effects on public finances beyond the immediate costs of the service in question. It is also worth noting that the effects can both pull towards lower and higher costs after any dynamic effects. The Ministry of Finance has no fundamental opposition to taking into account the behavioural effects of public expenditure. If there is sufficient empirical basis for specific headings, the effects should be taken into account as a starting point. A higher educational tendency thus results in both labour supply effects as well as higher occupational participation and working time, as well as increased productivity in the Ministry of Finance's calculations. The fact that the Ministry of Finance recognises behavioural effects in the form of increased labour supply of tax measures is due to the fact that, in accordance with current accounting principles, it is not only theoretically justified but also empirically substantiated. For many political and economic measures, they can have opposite behavior effects. If we take the effects of a tax relief, there are both effects that are pulling towards an increase in labour supply and effects that are pulling towards a reduction in labour supply. A tax reduction on the one hand gives a carrot in the form of an increased gain by working instead of taking time off, the so-called substitution effect, that is what advocates that we will work more. On the other hand, there is an effect that is drawing on us to work less because, as a result of tax cuts, we do not have to work as much to achieve the same consumption as we have hitherto, the so-called income effect or more popular: the hammock effect. Both of these effects are included when the Ministry of Finance recognises the behavioural effects of tax changes. From empirical studies it is estimated that the substitution effect is greater than the income effect. Therefore, tax relief is expected to lead to an increase in labour supply. I think that the proposers completely overlook the fact that the effects of public service actions can also have counterproductive effects, for example. on the supply of work. I agree that it is right to assume that some of the costs of, for example, child care or health improves the ability of citizens to take care of a job and can therefore have positive effects on the work supply. However, when public services are provided cheaply or free of charge to citizens, this may at the same time lead to some working less because of the so-called income or hammock effect. I would like to comment on why it is practically more difficult to assess the behavioural effects of changes in public expenditure than it is for behavioural changes in tax changes.
