The draft law proposes three measures: to establish a definition of an evaluation model for public procurement, to increase transparency for tenderers and to ensure that public contracting entities do not break the mark before assessing the quality of individual tenders. The Procurement Directive does not define the concept of an evaluation model and therefore there has not been a definition of this in the Procurement Act in the past. With the bill, the government will then introduce the concept of an evaluation model, which is defined as, and I quote from the bill: "The tool (s) used by the contracting entity to bring together the tenderers' response to different award criteria in order to identify the most economically advantageous tender'. I think it is quite sensible to introduce a definition into Danish law, although there is no definition in the Procurement Directive. Then there is something about transparency, because it is important. The Procurement Act has an obligation to publish evaluation methods, but it follows from the practice of the Procurement Board that this obligation does not preclude parts of the evaluation model from being exempted from publication in certain situations. With this change of law and the bill, the government will bring the legal situation back to the intention of the Procurement Act, so that contracting entities in the tender documentation are obliged to determine and describe the content of all parts of the evaluation model. In concrete terms, contracting entities are required to describe in detail the tools used to compare tenders and to find a winner of a tender. This strengthens the ex ante transparency of tenderers and, at the same time, supports equal treatment of tenderers. Tenderers are thus expected to have better opportunities to optimize their offer, which must be said to be quite good. In practice, the obligation to publish would mean that it would not be legal for contracting entities to use parts of an evaluation model if those parts had not been described in the tender dossier. Then there is a duty to assess the quality of tenders received separately from the price assessment. Indeed, this is something that the draft law has to solve, that is, the problem that if a contracting entity knows the price of the offer at the time when the quality of the offer is to be assessed, there may be a risk that this will have an unintentional spill-over effect on the quality assessment. Offers at a very low price are currently at risk of achieving a better quality assessment than what seems reasonable, because the price has become more important in the evaluation phase than when the supply was designed. It is therefore proposed to amend the Procurement Act in order to introduce an obligation to assess the quality of tenders received separately from the assessment of the price of works contracts with an estimated value of more than 350 million. kr., when contracts are awarded according to the criterion best price-quality ratio.
