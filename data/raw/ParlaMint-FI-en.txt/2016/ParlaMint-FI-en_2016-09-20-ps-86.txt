In this budget debate, the government was faced with a choice: are we taking decisions to increase employment and increase incentives for work and entrepreneurship, or are we going to continue along the path of sustained cuts and savings? High employment and a welfare society are an integral pair. That is why the government has decided to give priority to employment in all decision-making. However, we are still a long way from the target set for the Government Programme. Finland is not yet in the right shape with these measures and with these prospects. We are not yet reaching a level of employment that will enable us to secure lasting prosperity for future generations. In fact, the government is working in a very challenging situation. Our export-dependent economy is not at its best — our competitiveness has been allowed to decline for years. This is why Finnish products do not sell in the world. At the same time, globalisation, automation, the redistribution of the economy are challenging us from the outside. However, economic indicators indicate that the direction of government decisions is the right one. We have managed to reverse the direction of economic forecasts from a negative to a cautiously positive one after a long recession. According to the Ministry of Finance forecast, gradually improving employment, low inflation and significant tax reductions in labour support consumption and future faith. This development has been reinforced in particular by the creation of a competitiveness pact, reforms in business taxation and labour taxation, and market-opening reforms such as the liberalisation of trading hours. In fact, this morning we received the latest employment figures which would still show a slight improvement in the employment situation — good. This year, however, and in the years to come, growth will only be around 1%. That's not enough for us. According to the forecast, in 18, our total output will be almost 3% lower than in 2008. Thus, Finland will soon have lost a decade in terms of increasing well-being. In August, I received a report by three prestigious economists, Sixten Korkman, Vesa Vihriälä and Bengt Holmström, on the need for economic policy. In their report, professors note that the solutions made are likely to break the debt trend in the next few years, but, on the other hand, economic policy is not such as to reduce domestic demand. The message of economists is in fact quite clear: the basic impetus of the government's economic line is correct. But the equally clear message was that we need more structural reforms in order to achieve growth and to improve employment. The recommendations of economists are precisely the means already available in our government programme: reducing the tax burden on labour, improving the conditions and competitiveness of business, reducing the thresholds for employment, reducing unemployment security, reforming the education system, deregulation and increasing competition. Although the government has decided to cut public spending by EUR 4 billion over four years, our public spending is still growing at a worryingly high pace. Therefore, the Government’s aim is only to curb the increase in expenditure at this stage and in this situation. The growth in our expenditure is mainly due to the growing need for social and health services in Finland as the population ages. In addition, unemployment results in a fall of billions in public finances. It is high time to say jointly that the recovery with taxpayers' money will not save Finland. Finland's state and municipalities have been in debt since 2009 at an annual rate of approximately EUR 9 billion. So between 2009 and 16, we have spent some EUR 70 billion more than we have earned. All the parties in this Chamber have been in charge of the government during that time. The debt recovery intended to compensate for cyclical fluctuations will not solve the problems arising from the restructuring of the Finnish economy. Therefore, the Government aims to improve competitiveness and employment, solve structural problems, make taxation more supportive and build a better environment for Finnish companies to create economic growth and work in Finland. Every Finn will benefit from this. Mr President! The Government's recent budget proposal is a responsible whole, focused on economic reform through the strengthening of employment. In addition, the budget maintains a policy of economic policy that curbs expenditure growth. Central government expenditure will amount to EUR 55.2 billion next year and revenue to EUR 49.7 billion. The shortfall in income is covered by a debt of EUR 5.5 billion. So the government is still in debt at roughly the same rate as it was this year. The rate of debt is slowing down towards the end of the spending limits period as the government's decisions begin to be effective with full force. For the sake of certainty, I would also like to say to the representatives of the SDP, for example, who, in turn, have horrified both the savings and the growth in debt, that fast-paced deleveraging would mean tighter austerity lists and risking our delicate economic growth.
