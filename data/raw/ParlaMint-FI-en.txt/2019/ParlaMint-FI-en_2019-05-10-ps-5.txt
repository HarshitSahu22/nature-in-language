A distinguished debate has begun here on the nature of Finnish political decision-making, but if we are dealing here with a strong lobbying phenomenon and corporateism as some sort of eternal Finnish feature, let us remind you that when Sipilä's government was formed at the Regulation House, we did something quite differently. After all, there were no lobbyists invited and we, i.e. representatives of parliament, and officials and scientists, were set up there. [ Hustles from the left] More generally, this phenomenon, that is, Finland's organizational drive, has to be reversed for a long time. It was born during the Cold War, when there might have been some need for it, but it has remained to live in a way that separates us from our Nordic and European neighbours in a way that many people in Finland really do not know. Finland is by far the most corporatist country in the Nordic countries, in other words, where the constitution on many issues is, in fact, only a nominal matter and organisations that exercise real power. No other region has the same negative effects as employment policy. As you well know, Mr President, when social democracy and the trade union movement in the last century, during the old devaluation policy, were managing wage moderation, the trade union movement was performing reasonably well, but now that we are in an age of ageing, when we need to reform the supply of labour, in other words, with all possible means of getting more people into the labour market, the trade union movement has not been able to be a constructive force in any country that would have taken these reforms forward. I say this with a certain longing mind, because I have worked for a large part of my career in the trade union movement and its research institutes, but the experience in the Nordic countries and similar countries is that when you have to reform the supply of work, the trade union movement has been against them. Just think of the 2011-2015 term of government, when the Minister of Labour was in fact the former chairman of the representative organisation and under his leadership, so that any reform of which the government parties had agreed — the coalition and the Social Democrats agreed on the various reforms — if this reform did not receive the green light from the organisations, it did not progress, even if it had increased employment. We will never be able to make a positive contribution in terms of employment — that is to say, by giving an organisation the right of veto. After all, the sad harvest of its reign was the fall in employment by over a hundred thousand. I don't know if they cared about this at all. It is not by chance that the most corporatist, or organizational, country in the Nordic countries is also the Nordic country with the highest unemployment and, in fact, almost the highest unemployment in northern Europe.
