However, based on preliminary data published by Statistics Finland a couple of weeks ago, economic growth has been more positive than forecast, as GDP grew by 2.2 per cent in July to September from the third quarter of 2018. Exports also grew by over five per cent from one year ago. Unfortunately, the new financial forecast from the Ministry of Finance does not come until tomorrow. It would have been a good source of inspiration for this debate. The draft budget estimates an increase in the number of employed persons and an increase in the employment rate to 73.4% by 2021. In October this year the employment rate was already 72.7 per cent and the unemployment rate 6.7 per cent. This draft budget proposed a budget of EUR 57.7 billion, which is about EUR 2.1 billion more than last year's actual budget. The expenditure level is raised, in particular, by the permanent expenditure increases included in the Government Programme and fixed-term future investments. Some so-called automatic factors, such as growth in age-related central government expenditure, full index increases and compensation for the impact of tax base changes on municipalities, are also reflected as an increase in expenditure compared to this year. On the other hand, a good employment situation will reduce unemployment security expenditure. Similarly, interest payments do not increase but rather decrease. It is estimated that tax revenue will grow relatively cautiously by 2.7 per cent next year. These parts of mathematics leave the draft budget with a deficit of around EUR 2.2 billion, which will be covered by additional debt. This, of course, must also be related to the international situation and to the control group. Finland is still among the most indebted euro economies. The Committee stresses the measures taken to balance the economy in accordance with the objectives set by 2023 at the latest. This requires, above all, an increase in the employment rate to at least 75%. In addition, the implementation of structural reforms must continue and the conditions for economic growth must be improved by stimulating investment in knowledge and innovation. Of this EUR 1.4 billion increase in expenditure for the entire parliamentary term up to 2020, approximately EUR 1.1 billion will be allocated to social security and social and health services, early childhood education, education and research, environmental protection and climate policy measures. Such a social policy is an exception, and perhaps this is why it is worth reminding ourselves of what is being done with that money. In order to reduce poverty and non-participation, cuts and obligations of the active model of unemployment benefit will be withdrawn from the beginning of next year, the basic security will also be increased by around EUR 20 from the beginning of next year, and maintenance support will be increased in order to ensure the livelihood of families with children, the maintenance allowance will be increased, child allowances for the fourth and fifth children will be increased, and the single parent's increase in the child benefit will also benefit families dependent on income support, from the beginning of next year. In order to reduce the poverty of pensioners, small pensions will be increased through national pension and guarantee pension schemes, 50 guaranteed pension at the beginning of next year and, as a rule, 34 euros per month of national pension, and normal index adjustments will be made throughout the line. This is a significant investment and a clear change of course to the previous one. The permanent expenditure increases will mainly be covered by decision-based additional revenue and reallocations, and the financing base will be strengthened through taxation by around EUR 700 million. At the same time, tax solutions aim at contributing to climate objectives, reducing inequalities and reducing income disparities. These one-off future investments of up to EUR 3 billion during the parliamentary term have sparked debate. It is a new type of mechanism and it has decided to invest EUR 1.4 billion and to experiment with social importance between 20 and 22 years. Of these, EUR 0.75 billion will be presented for adoption for 2020. Of these, the most significant investments are in vocational training, improving the quality and equality of basic education, compensatory allowances for natural handicaps and subsidies from Business Finland for the research, development and investment package. The Committee considers these contributions to be justified and stresses that they enable investment which increases the potential of the economy or contributes to achieving significant social objectives. In particular, efforts are needed to develop the transport network, housing and a vibrant Finland, as well as to achieve the objectives of carbon neutrality. It is important that the government has launched a large-scale preparatory work on employment with the aim of drawing up proposals by spring 20 for measures to increase the number of people employed by 30,000 in the first phase. The Committee stresses the diversity of measures, since no single reform is sufficient to improve employment, but a number of measures are needed in the same direction. Investments in research, science and knowledge are a key prerequisite for Finland’s success, competitiveness and well-being. The committee therefore stresses the implementation of the policy on improving the R & D environment contained in the Government Programme. As I mentioned earlier, the Committee also stresses that achieving carbon neutrality in Finland by 35 will require major changes in consumption and mobility patterns. Intersectoral measures are needed in several administrative sectors, and in particular solutions to promote the circular and bioeconomy. It is also essential to ensure the consistency and compatibility of these measures. The committee also considers it essential that the business support system be developed more cost-effective for central government finances and that climate policy objectives be taken into account.
